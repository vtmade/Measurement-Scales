# Content Validity

## Overview

**Content validity** addresses the fundamental question: **"Does the scale cover everything it should cover?"**

This is the foundation of all scale validation. If your items don't adequately represent the construct, no amount of statistical analysis can fix the problem.

## What is Content Validity?

Content validity is the degree to which the items in a scale:
1. **Comprehensively represent** all facets of the construct
2. **Are relevant** to the construct being measured
3. **Are clear and unambiguous** in meaning
4. **Don't include irrelevant content** or unnecessary items

Unlike other forms of validity, content validity is primarily **judgmental** rather than statistical. It relies on expert evaluation and theoretical reasoning.

## The Content Validity Process

### Phase 1: Construct Definition & Domain Specification

**Goal**: Clearly define what you're measuring and its boundaries

#### Step 1: Define Your Construct
- Write a clear, detailed definition
- Specify what IS included in the construct
- Specify what is NOT included (boundaries)
- Ground definition in theory and literature

**Example: Job Satisfaction**
```
Definition: "An employee's overall positive or negative evaluative
judgment about their job and work environment."

Included:
- Feelings about work tasks
- Perceptions of work environment
- Attitudes toward job features

NOT included:
- Life satisfaction (too broad)
- Organizational commitment (different construct)
- Momentary mood states (too temporary)
```

#### Step 2: Identify Dimensions/Facets
- Break the construct into key dimensions
- Use theory, literature reviews, and qualitative research
- Decide if the scale is unidimensional or multidimensional

**Example: Job Satisfaction Dimensions**
1. Satisfaction with work itself
2. Satisfaction with pay and benefits
3. Satisfaction with supervision
4. Satisfaction with coworkers
5. Satisfaction with promotion opportunities
6. Satisfaction with working conditions

### Phase 2: Item Generation

**Goal**: Create a comprehensive pool of potential items

#### Sources for Item Generation

1. **Literature Review**
   - Review existing scales (but don't copy items verbatim due to copyright)
   - Identify gaps in existing measures
   - Note how others have operationalized similar constructs

2. **Theory**
   - Use theoretical frameworks to guide item content
   - Ensure items map onto theoretical components

3. **Qualitative Research**
   - Interviews with target population
   - Focus groups
   - Critical incident technique
   - Content analysis of open-ended responses

4. **Expert Input**
   - Subject matter experts
   - Experienced researchers in the domain

#### Item Writing Guidelines

**DO:**
- ✅ Write 2-3x more items than you need (trim later)
- ✅ Use simple, clear language
- ✅ Write items at appropriate reading level for target population
- ✅ Use consistent response format
- ✅ Balance positively and negatively worded items (carefully)
- ✅ Make each item represent one idea

**DON'T:**
- ❌ Use jargon or technical terms (unless appropriate for population)
- ❌ Write double-barreled items ("I am satisfied with my pay and coworkers")
- ❌ Use double negatives ("I am not unsatisfied...")
- ❌ Make items too specific or too general
- ❌ Include leading or loaded language

**Example Item Pool: Job Satisfaction (Initial)**
```
For each dimension, generate 8-10 items = 48-60 total items

Work Itself:
- "I find my work meaningful"
- "My job tasks are interesting"
- "I enjoy the type of work I do"
- "My work allows me to use my skills"
- [etc.]

Pay & Benefits:
- "I am satisfied with my current salary"
- "My compensation is fair for my responsibilities"
- "The benefits package meets my needs"
- [etc.]
```

### Phase 3: Expert Review

**Goal**: Evaluate item relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness

#### Expert Panel Selection
- **How many?** 5-10 experts minimum
- **Who?** Mix of:
  - Academic researchers in the field
  - Practitioners with subject matter expertise
  - Measurement/psychometrics experts

#### Expert Review Tasks

**Task 1: Relevance Rating**
For each item, experts rate: "How relevant is this item to [construct]?"

Scale:
```
1 = Not relevant
2 = Somewhat relevant
3 = Quite relevant
4 = Highly relevant
```

**Task 2: Clarity Rating**
"How clear and unambiguous is this item?"
```
1 = Not clear
2 = Somewhat clear
3 = Quite clear
4 = Very clear
```

**Task 3: Dimension Assignment**
"Which dimension does this item belong to?"
- Forces experts to classify items
- Reveals if items map to intended dimensions

**Task 4: Qualitative Feedback**
- Suggest revisions
- Identify missing content
- Flag problematic items

#### Calculating Content Validity Metrics

**Content Validity Ratio (CVR)** - Lawshe, 1975
```
CVR = (ne - N/2) / (N/2)

Where:
ne = number of experts rating item as "essential"
N = total number of experts

CVR ranges from -1 to +1
Positive values indicate > 50% agreement
```

**Minimum CVR values** (depend on panel size):
- 5 experts: CVR ≥ 0.99
- 7 experts: CVR ≥ 0.99
- 10 experts: CVR ≥ 0.62
- 15 experts: CVR ≥ 0.49

**Content Validity Index (CVI)** - Lynn, 1986
```
Item-CVI (I-CVI) =
  Number of experts rating 3 or 4 / Total experts

Scale-CVI (S-CVI) =
  Average of all I-CVI values

Guidelines:
I-CVI ≥ 0.78 for 6+ experts
S-CVI ≥ 0.90 desirable
```

### Phase 4: Item Revision & Selection

Based on expert review, take action:

#### Keep Items That:
- ✅ High relevance ratings (≥ 3.0 mean)
- ✅ High clarity ratings (≥ 3.0 mean)
- ✅ Strong expert agreement (CVR/CVI above threshold)
- ✅ Correctly classified into intended dimension

#### Revise Items That:
- ⚠️ Moderate relevance but low clarity → Reword
- ⚠️ Disagreement about dimension → Clarify or reassign
- ⚠️ Good concept but poor wording → Revise based on feedback

#### Delete Items That:
- ❌ Low relevance ratings
- ❌ Consistent expert agreement to remove
- ❌ Redundant with other items
- ❌ Cannot be revised adequately

**Typical Attrition**: 60 initial items → 35-40 after expert review

### Phase 5: Cognitive Interviews (Recommended)

**Goal**: Test if respondents interpret items as intended

#### Process:
1. Recruit 5-10 people from target population
2. Have them complete scale while "thinking aloud"
3. Ask follow-up questions:
   - "What does this item mean to you?"
   - "How did you decide your answer?"
   - "Was anything confusing?"

#### Common Issues Found:
- Items interpreted differently than intended
- Difficult or ambiguous wording
- Response scale confusion
- Missing important content

#### Outcome:
- Final item revisions
- Confirmation that items work as intended
- Ready for pilot testing

## Documentation Requirements

For publication, report:

1. **Construct Definition**
   - Clear operational definition
   - Theoretical grounding
   - Domain specification

2. **Item Development Process**
   - Sources used (literature, theory, qualitative)
   - Initial item pool size
   - Item writing procedures

3. **Expert Review**
   - Expert qualifications
   - Number of experts
   - Procedures used
   - Quantitative results (CVR/CVI)
   - How feedback was incorporated

4. **Cognitive Testing** (if conducted)
   - Sample characteristics
   - Key findings
   - Item modifications

## Examples from Published Research

### Example 1: Emotional Intelligence Scale (Petrides & Furnham, 2001)

**Process:**
1. Reviewed 15 existing EI models and measures
2. Identified 13 distinct facets of trait EI
3. Generated 160 items covering all facets
4. Expert panel (n=7) rated relevance
5. Reduced to 144 items based on CVR
6. Cognitive interviews with 20 participants
7. Final scale: 153 items across 13 facets

**Content Validity Evidence:**
- Comprehensive literature review
- Explicit mapping of items to facets
- Expert validation
- User testing for clarity

### Example 2: Workplace Incivility Scale (Cortina et al., 2001)

**Process:**
1. Defined incivility via literature and theory
2. Generated 20 items from qualitative interviews
3. Expert panel (n=5 academics, 3 HR professionals)
4. All items had I-CVI > 0.80
5. Pilot tested with 50 employees
6. Final scale: 7 items (13 deleted for redundancy/low clarity)

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Skipping Construct Definition
**Problem**: Items lack coherent focus
**Solution**: Always start with clear definition and domain specification

### Pitfall 2: Too Few Initial Items
**Problem**: Can't adequately cover construct after item reduction
**Solution**: Generate 2-3x items you need in final scale

### Pitfall 3: Using Unqualified "Experts"
**Problem**: Poor quality feedback
**Solution**: Select experts with genuine expertise in content domain

### Pitfall 4: Ignoring Expert Feedback
**Problem**: Retaining poor items due to attachment
**Solution**: Use objective criteria (CVR/CVI) to make decisions

### Pitfall 5: No Cognitive Testing
**Problem**: Items misunderstood by respondents
**Solution**: Always test with actual target population

## Quick Reference Checklist

Before moving to statistical validation, ensure you have:

- [ ] Clear construct definition grounded in theory
- [ ] Comprehensive literature review completed
- [ ] Item pool 2-3x larger than needed
- [ ] Expert review with 5+ qualified experts
- [ ] Quantitative content validity evidence (CVR/CVI)
- [ ] Items revised based on expert feedback
- [ ] Cognitive interviews with target population
- [ ] Documentation of entire process
- [ ] Final item pool ready for pilot testing
