# Best Practices & Guidelines

## Overview

This guide provides **practical guidance** for common decisions and challenges in scale development and validation. Use these evidence-based recommendations to make informed choices throughout your validation process.

---

## When to Use Existing vs. Develop New Scales

### Use an Existing Validated Scale When:

✅ **A good measure already exists**
- The construct is well-established
- Existing scale has strong psychometric properties
- Scale fits your population and context
- You want to compare findings across studies

✅ **You have limited resources**
- Time constraints
- Budget limitations
- Small team

✅ **You're not a measurement specialist**
- Validation requires specific expertise
- Don't have training in psychometrics
- Risk of creating poor measure

✅ **Cumulative science is the goal**
- Build on prior research
- Enable meta-analyses
- Facilitate comparison across studies

**Benefits:**
- Known psychometric properties
- Established norms and benchmarks
- Literature using the same measure
- Less time and cost
- Defensible choice

### Develop a New Scale When:

✅ **No adequate measure exists**
- Construct is novel
- Existing scales don't match your definition
- Gaps in available measures

✅ **Existing scales don't fit your context**
- Wrong population (e.g., children vs. adults)
- Wrong culture/language
- Wrong setting (e.g., clinical vs. organizational)

✅ **Theory has evolved**
- New conceptualization of construct
- Existing scales outdated
- Theoretical refinement needed

✅ **Existing scales have serious flaws**
- Poor psychometric properties
- Validity concerns
- Overly long or impractical

✅ **You have adequate resources**
- Time (1-3 years)
- Budget ($5,000-30,000+)
- Expertise in scale development
- Access to samples

**Requirements:**
- Strong theoretical foundation
- Methodological expertise
- Multiple samples for validation
- Commitment to rigorous validation

## The "Shortcut" Version (Minimal Validation)

### When Minimal Validation is Acceptable

**Appropriate for:**
- Pilot studies / exploratory research
- Dissertations with limited scope
- Using established scale in slightly new context
- Internal organizational use (not publication)

### Minimum Acceptable Standards

Even with shortcuts, you **MUST** have:

1. **✅ Use existing validated scales** whenever possible
   - Cite original validation studies
   - Report psychometrics from your sample

2. **✅ Check reliability in YOUR sample**
   - Calculate and report Cronbach's α
   - Minimum α ≥ .70
   - Report if reliability differs from original

3. **✅ Run basic factor analysis**
   - At minimum: EFA to confirm structure
   - Check if factor structure holds in your sample
   - Report deviations from original

4. **✅ Report any modifications** honestly
   - If you change items: explain why
   - If you adapt for population: describe adaptation
   - Acknowledge limitations

### What You Can Skip (Temporarily)

In early-stage research only:
- ⚠️ CFA (but run EFA)
- ⚠️ Test-retest reliability (if measuring stable constructs)
- ⚠️ Extensive nomological network testing
- ⚠️ Cross-validation in multiple samples

**Critical:** Acknowledge these limitations in your write-up

### Example Text for Minimal Validation

> "We used the established Job Satisfaction Scale (Smith & Jones, 2015).
> The original validation demonstrated strong psychometric properties
> (α = .89, N = 500). In our sample (N = 145), internal consistency was
> acceptable (α = .76). Exploratory factor analysis confirmed the expected
> three-factor structure, with all items loading > .40 on their intended
> factors. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, more extensive
> validation was not conducted."

## Why "Making Up" Response Scales Is Problematic

### The Problem

When you create response choices "on the fly" without validation:

❌ **No evidence items measure what you think**
- Respondents may interpret differently than intended
- No proof of construct validity

❌ **No idea if it's reliable**
- Could be measuring random error
- Findings won't replicate

❌ **No basis for comparing to other research**
- Can't relate to existing literature
- Undermines cumulative science

❌ **No confidence in conclusions**
- Don't know if effects are real or measurement artifacts
- Threats to internal and external validity

### The Solution

**Option 1:** Use existing validated scales (best)
**Option 2:** Conduct proper validation (if new scale needed)
**Option 3:** Acknowledge limitations (if using unvalidated measure)

### If You Must Use an Unvalidated Measure

**Be honest about limitations:**

> "Due to [constraints], we developed ad-hoc items to measure [construct].
> This measure has not been formally validated. Findings should be interpreted
> cautiously and require replication with validated instruments. Cronbach's
> alpha in our sample was .72, suggesting acceptable internal consistency,
> but validity evidence is lacking."

**Then:**
- Report internal consistency
- Run factor analysis to check dimensionality
- Correlate with related measures (if available)
- Clearly label as exploratory

## Adapting Existing Scales

### When Adaptation is Needed

**Common scenarios:**
- Translating to new language
- Adapting for different age group
- Modifying for different context (e.g., school → workplace)
- Shortening a long scale

### Minor Adaptations (Require Limited Revalidation)

**Examples:**
- Changing "employees" to "students"
- Updating outdated terminology
- Adjusting reading level

**Required:**
1. Check content validity (do changes preserve meaning?)
2. Verify reliability in your sample (α)
3. Confirm factor structure (EFA or CFA)
4. Test key validity relationships

### Major Adaptations (Require Full Revalidation)

**Examples:**
- Translation to new language
- Significant item rewording
- Different response format
- Different construct definition

**Required:**
- Full validation process (as if new scale)
- Translation/back-translation (if language change)
- Measurement invariance testing
- Cross-cultural validation (if relevant)

### Translation Best Practices

**Process:**
1. **Forward translation** (original → target language by 2+ translators)
2. **Reconciliation** (resolve differences)
3. **Back-translation** (target → original by independent translator)
4. **Expert review** (compare back-translation to original)
5. **Cognitive interviews** (test comprehension in target population)
6. **Pilot testing** (assess psychometric properties)
7. **Full validation** (EFA, CFA, reliability, validity)

**Test measurement invariance:**
- Configural invariance (same structure)
- Metric invariance (same loadings)
- Scalar invariance (same intercepts)

## Creating Short Forms

### Why Shorten a Scale?

**Legitimate reasons:**
- Reduce respondent burden
- Increase survey response rates
- Make scale more practical
- Improve efficiency without losing validity

### How to Create a Short Form

#### Method 1: Rational/Theoretical Selection
1. Identify essential facets
2. Select best items per facet (highest loadings)
3. Balance content coverage
4. Retain theoretical diversity

#### Method 2: Empirical Selection
1. Rank items by factor loadings
2. Select highest-loading items
3. Check for redundancy (inter-item correlations)
4. Ensure coverage of construct

#### Method 3: Hybrid (Recommended)
1. Use both theoretical and empirical criteria
2. Consider practical constraints
3. Cognitive testing for retained items
4. Revalidate short form

### Standards for Short Forms

**A good short form should:**
- Correlate r ≥ .90 with long form
- Maintain α ≥ .70 (ideally ≥ .80)
- Preserve factor structure
- Show similar validity evidence
- Cover all key facets (if multidimensional)

**Minimum items:**
- Single factor: 3-4 items minimum
- Multiple factors: 3+ items per factor

**Example:** Grit-S (8 items) vs. Grit-O (12 items)
- Correlation: r = .95
- α: .73-.83 (comparable)
- Validity: similar patterns
- Benefit: 33% shorter

## Balancing Brevity and Validity

### The Trade-Off

**Shorter scales:**
- ✅ Less respondent burden
- ✅ Higher completion rates
- ✅ More practical
- ❌ Lower reliability
- ❌ Less content coverage
- ❌ Reduced precision

**Longer scales:**
- ✅ Higher reliability
- ✅ Better content coverage
- ✅ More precision
- ❌ Respondent fatigue
- ❌ Lower response rates
- ❌ Less practical

### Decision Guidelines

**Go shorter (4-6 items) when:**
- Construct is narrow/focused
- Part of larger survey battery
- Screening/brief assessment
- Frequent assessment (diary studies)

**Go longer (10-20 items) when:**
- Construct is broad/complex
- Primary outcome measure
- High-stakes decisions (clinical, selection)
- Detailed assessment needed

**Typical range:** 5-15 items for most research scales

## Dealing with Common Challenges

### Challenge 1: Low Reliability (α < .70)

**Possible causes:**
- Too few items
- Heterogeneous construct
- Poor items
- Restricted range in sample

**Solutions:**
1. Add more items (if possible)
2. Check item-total correlations → remove poor items
3. Ensure items measure same construct
4. Check for data issues (coding errors, restricted range)
5. Consider if construct is truly unidimensional

### Challenge 2: Poor Model Fit in CFA

**Possible causes:**
- Wrong factor structure
- Cross-loadings
- Correlated errors
- Poor items

**Solutions:**
1. Review modification indices (theoretically justified changes only)
2. Allow correlated errors (if items share method variance)
3. Remove problematic items
4. Test alternative models
5. Consider if EFA structure was wrong

**Don't:**
- Make data-driven changes without theory
- Over-fit to single sample
- Ignore poor fit

### Challenge 3: Lack of Discriminant Validity

**Problem:** High correlations with supposedly different constructs

**Solutions:**
1. Review construct definitions (are they truly distinct?)
2. Check if items overlap in content
3. Rewrite items to be more specific
4. Use method variance controls (different sources)
5. Consider if constructs are actually separate

**May need to:**
- Reconceptualize constructs
- Combine overlapping scales
- Acknowledge limitations

### Challenge 4: Low Response Rates

**Strategies to improve:**
1. Shorten survey (use brief, validated measures)
2. Offer incentives
3. Multiple contact attempts
4. Explain purpose and importance
5. Ensure confidentiality/anonymity
6. Mobile-friendly format
7. Reduce cognitive burden

### Challenge 5: Small Sample Size

**If you can't get N > 200:**

**Options:**
1. Use existing validated scale (don't validate new one)
2. Focus on reliability and basic validity (not CFA)
3. Use Item Response Theory (requires smaller N)
4. Combine samples (if appropriate)
5. Collaborate with other researchers

**Acknowledge:**
- Limited statistical power
- Unstable parameter estimates
- Need for replication

## Ethical Considerations

### Responsible Scale Use

**Do:**
- ✅ Use validated measures when available
- ✅ Report reliability from your sample
- ✅ Acknowledge limitations
- ✅ Share measures for replication
- ✅ Respect copyright (get permission)

**Don't:**
- ❌ Present unvalidated measures as validated
- ❌ Selectively report only good results
- ❌ Use scales outside their validated context
- ❌ Violate test copyright
- ❌ Make high-stakes decisions with unvalidated measures

### Transparency

**Report:**
- All measures used (even if results non-significant)
- Any adaptations or modifications
- Psychometric properties in your sample
- Limitations of measurement

### Open Science

**Consider:**
- Pre-registering validation plans
- Sharing data and materials
- Open-access publication
- Making scales freely available (when appropriate)

## Domain-Specific Considerations

### Organizational/Workplace Scales

**Unique considerations:**
- Social desirability bias
- Organizational context matters
- Supervisor vs. self-ratings
- Job-specific vs. general measures

**Best practices:**
- Ensure confidentiality
- Use multi-source data when possible
- Consider organizational level of construct
- Test in multiple organizations

### Clinical/Diagnostic Scales

**Unique considerations:**
- High-stakes decisions
- Need for sensitivity and specificity
- Screening vs. diagnosis
- Change sensitivity (treatment response)

**Best practices:**
- Higher reliability standards (α ≥ .90)
- Criterion validity against gold standard
- Establish clinical cut-scores
- Test in clinical populations

### Educational Scales

**Unique considerations:**
- Developmental appropriateness
- Reading level
- Parent vs. teacher vs. self-report
- Academic vs. social-emotional

**Best practices:**
- Age-appropriate language and format
- Cognitive interviews with students
- Multi-informant when possible
- Test across grade levels

### Cross-Cultural Research

**Unique considerations:**
- Conceptual equivalence across cultures
- Translation challenges
- Cultural appropriateness
- Measurement invariance

**Best practices:**
- Consult cultural experts
- Rigorous translation process
- Test within each culture
- Assess measurement invariance

## Quick Decision Trees

### "Should I use an existing scale or create a new one?"

```
Does a validated scale exist for your construct?
├─ Yes → Does it fit your population/context?
│   ├─ Yes → USE EXISTING SCALE ✅
│   └─ No → Can you adapt it?
│       ├─ Yes → ADAPT & REVALIDATE
│       └─ No → CREATE NEW SCALE
└─ No → Do you have resources for validation?
    ├─ Yes → CREATE NEW SCALE
    └─ No → Reconsider research approach
```

### "How much validation do I need?"

```
What's your purpose?
├─ Exploratory research / pilot study
│   → MINIMAL validation (α + EFA)
├─ Journal publication
│   → STANDARD validation (EFA + CFA + validity)
├─ Scale development paper
│   → COMPREHENSIVE validation (multiple studies)
└─ High-stakes use (clinical, selection)
    → GOLD STANDARD validation (extensive evidence)
```

### "Is my reliability acceptable?"

```
What's your Cronbach's alpha?
├─ α < .60 → UNACCEPTABLE - revise or abandon
├─ α = .60-.69 → QUESTIONABLE - improve if possible
├─ α = .70-.79 → ACCEPTABLE - okay for research
├─ α = .80-.89 → GOOD - standard for most uses
└─ α ≥ .90 → EXCELLENT - but check for redundancy
```

## Final Recommendations

### Top 10 Best Practices

1. **Use existing validated scales** whenever possible
2. **Always report reliability** in your sample
3. **Use multiple samples** for validation (EFA ≠ CFA sample)
4. **Collect both convergent and discriminant validity** evidence
5. **Be transparent** about limitations
6. **Don't skip content validity** (expert review, cognitive testing)
7. **Report complete statistics** (not just "good fit")
8. **Consider practical utility** (length, ease of use)
9. **Plan for adequate sample sizes** (N ≥ 200 for factor analysis)
10. **Think long-term** (scales should serve the field, not just your study)
